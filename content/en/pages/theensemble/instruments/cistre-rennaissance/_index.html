16th & 17th centuries, Europe

body: >-


With its flat back, metal strings and a sound that catches the ear, the cittern is an instrument with real character! Probably descended from the medieval citole, this plucked string instrument differs from the lute in both construction and tone. The cittern has a flat back, while the lute's rounded back is built from narrow wooden ribs joined together. Its brass or steel strings, often played with a plectrum, give the cittern its particularly bright, ringing tone.


Four strings? More precisely, many citterns have four courses , each a group of strings played together. The number varies from one instrument and tradition to another, with some citterns having six or seven courses. That familiar outline belongs to a whole family of instruments!


The cittern has fixed metal frets set into the fingerboard, while the lute has gut frets tied around the neck. The layout of the frets determines which notes can be played. Diatonic fretting leaves out some semitones, while chromatic fretting makes every semitone available in sequence. Both systems were in use as early as the 16th century. Some frets extend only partway across the fingerboard, a feature that directly affects which fingerings and chords are possible.


But the cittern's story extends well beyond the instrument maker's workshop. It was part of everyday life, even finding its way into English barbershops, where customers could play it while they waited their turn! That familiar role can make it easy to underestimate the demands of its repertoire. As much at home in good company as it was in the hands of a virtuoso, the cittern lent itself to intricate playing.


Instrument maker Ugo Casalonga built the ensemble a copy of a cittern made by Gasparo da Salò in Brescia in 1560. The original is now on display in the Hill Collection at the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford, UK. Casalonga's copy is made of figured maple, with a soundboard of spruce from the Jura.
