XVIIIth century, British Isles

body: >-


With its metal strings, flat back and misleading name, the English guitar is a member of the cittern family! Also known as the guittar , it became popular across the British Isles from the mid-18th century onwards. Despite its name, it differed from the gut-strung guitar of the time in both its construction and its bright, ringing tone.


Ten strings in six courses were the most common arrangement. Each of the two lowest courses had a single string, while the four upper courses each had a pair of strings played together. This gave the instrument single strings in the bass and paired strings in the upper register.


Its metal strings can bring to mind the sound of a modern steel-string acoustic guitar or an Irish bouzouki. The comparison gives a sense of its distinctive tone, though each instrument has its own history. In the ensemble, the English guitar provides chordal support and a rhythmic lift that brings out the movement of the dances.


Irish instrument maker Frank Tate built the ensemble's English guitar after an original made by William Gibson in Dublin in 1778 and now held at the National Museum of Ireland. We chose this model following research with Jean-Christophe Morel in several British and Irish collections, including those of the University of Edinburgh, the Robert Burns Museum, the Victoria and Albert Museum and the Royal Academy of Music.
